How a congestion episode becomes an emission cost

An interactive reading of Abbasi and Zhou (2026), From Bottleneck Episodes to Travel and Emission Costs: A Cross-Resolution Framework with Empirical Evaluation. Every number here is computed in your browser from the paper's frozen data; nothing is computed beyond the paper's own equations.

Vehicle-miles and vehicle-hours tell you how far and how long. They do not tell you how much was emitted, because emission rates are nonlinear in speed and acceleration. A bottleneck episode supplies what is missing: the queued state, its service rate, the delay, and the transitions into and out of the queue.

Part 1 · The missing information

The puzzle: same miles, same hours, different emissions

Take one vehicle on a link of length $L$ with free-flow speed $v_f$. It arrives with excess travel time $w$ (its delay). Two descriptions of the same trip are both consistent with the measured travel time $T_f+w$: it moved at one average speed throughout, or it ran at $v_f$ and then crawled through a queue at $v_q$. Predict first: which description emits more CO2, and does the answer depend on $v_q$?

The two-speed trip spends $T_{\rm queue}=v_f w/(v_f-v_q)$ in the queue and the rest at $v_f$. Because the rate per mile $f(v)=r(v)/v$ is convex at low speeds, the two-speed path emits more than the average-speed path for the same miles and hours, and the gap grows as $v_q$ falls. The gap is the information that VMT and VHT lose. The delay cannot exceed $L(1/v_q-1/v_f)$, or the vehicle would need more than the whole link to be queued: that is the finite-link condition, Eq. (9) of the paper.

Part 2 · Traffic states and the closed episode

Illustration: detector-78 fundamental diagram; Newell queue with adjustable values · Tutorial T1

Watch the queue state follow the service rate

On a triangular fundamental diagram with capacity $C$, free-flow speed $v_f$ and jam density $k_j$, a bottleneck serving at rate $\mu$ holds the queue in one congested state. The queued-vehicle speed is $v_q=\mu/(k_j-\mu/\omega)$, Eq. (2). It is not the wave speed and not the whole-link average speed. Slide $\mu$ toward capacity and watch $v_q$ rise toward $v_f$.

The closed episode: counting vehicles and delay

A closed fluid queue with constant service: arrivals exceed $\mu$ from onset $t_0$, the queue peaks at $t_2$ and clears at $t_3$. With Newell's quadratic arrival peak the queue is $Q(t)=\tfrac{b}{3}(t-t_0)^2(t_3-t)$, total delay is $W_P=\int Q\,dt=D\bar w_P$, and the mean delay is $9/16$ of the peak delay for this model, whatever $P$ and $\mu$ are; other closed profiles give other ratios. The paper's Eq. (4) says the time average of the delay profile equals the vehicle average, which is what the emission calculation needs.

Part 3 · The two-speed emission identity

Frozen output: detector-78 reference state of Appendix F (defaults reproduce Table F1) · Tutorial T2

From delay to emissions: the conversion factor Γ

With zero-acceleration rates $r(v)$ in g per vehicle-hour, a vehicle that spends its delay in the queued state emits $$e=e_0+\Gamma(v_q)\,w,\qquad e_0=r(v_f)T_f,\qquad \Gamma(v_q)=\frac{v_f r(v_q)-v_q r(v_f)}{v_f-v_q},$$ Eq. (8). Over a closed episode at a common queue state, $E_P=D[e_0+\Gamma(v_q)\bar w_P]$, Eq. (10), or equivalently $E_P=\frac{r(v_f)}{v_f}{\rm VMT}+\Gamma(v_q)\big({\rm VHT}-{\rm VMT}/v_f\big)$, Eq. (12). Nothing else about the delay profile matters for the total. The tiles reproduce Table F1 of the paper at the detector-78 reference state when the sliders are at their defaults.

Can you read it? Why is Γ negative for NOx?
$\Gamma$ is the slope of the chord of emission-per-mile $f(v)=r(v)/v$ between pace $1/v_f$ and pace $1/v_q$. For NOx the rate per mile is high at 64 mph and lower at 18 mph, so slowing from free flow to the queue reduces the per-mile emission: a negative increment relative to the free-flow baseline, while the total trip emission stays positive on the admitted domain (Fig. 11 of the paper).
Part 4 · Demand changes the state

Frozen output: calibrated average-weekday QVDF cards · Tutorial T3

Loading changes the queue speed, and with it Γ

A calibrated queueing-based volume-delay function ties cumulative episode demand $x=D/C$ (hours of capacity) to duration $P(x)=\max\{f_d x^n, x\}$ and peak delay $w_{t_2}=T_f f_p P^s$, Eqs. (13)-(14). Because $\mu=Cx/P(x)$ changes with loading, so does $v_q$ and so does $\Gamma$. The emission increment $\Delta e=T_f\alpha x^\beta\Gamma(x)$ has loading elasticity $\beta+\varepsilon_{\Gamma,x}$, Eq. (19): the delay elasticity $\beta=ns$ plus a pollutant term. Pick a calibrated card and move the loading.

Part 5 · Finite transitions

Illustration at the Appendix F reference state; transition kernel of Eq. (22) · Tutorial T4

Replace the instantaneous switch by a real deceleration

The two-speed path switches speed instantly. A finite transition of duration $T$ with the symmetric kernel $h(\xi)=3\xi^2-2\xi^3$ covers the same time and distance as half a transition at each speed, Eq. (22), so replacing the switch changes only the emission. For a cubic rate the speed-only change is exact and closed-form, $$\delta e=-\tfrac{9}{140}\,T\,(\Delta v)^2\,r''(\bar v),$$ Eq. (23). A vehicle must also reach $v_q$ and return to $v_f$ inside the link, which bounds the delays that admit the common correction, Eq. (26). Choose peak magnitudes and a link length; the band can be empty.

Your turn: find a link that admits the peak-delay vehicle
At the reference state (1.04 mi, 1.5 and 1.0 m/s²) the band is 0.31 to 1.42 min: the mean-delay vehicle (0.84 min) is admitted, the peak-delay vehicle (1.82 min) is not. Lengthening $L$ widens the band only when the delay is held fixed; in the calibrated model the delay itself scales with $T_f=L/v_f$, so the paper checks the corridor with its own observed delay instead (Section 6.4).

Part 8 · Where it applies

Frozen output: the three applicability calculations · Example 3

Applicability, counted

The two-speed baseline needs a link that can hold the queue (finite-link condition); the common finite-transition correction additionally needs room for both transitions. On the short PeMS links the band of Eq. (26) is empty at the illustrative magnitudes, so the correction applies to a minority of the two-speed-admitted cohorts. The corridor representation, with its own observed delay, admits more of the long Arizona episodes than the single link. These are applicability conditions, not failures of the baseline.
